Glock 17,
taken apart

Deutsch-Wagram, Austria · 1982

A first pistol,
built to a deadline

Gaston Glock ran a small firm making curtain rods and polymer fittings outside Vienna and had never designed a firearm when, in 1980, he heard the Austrian army was looking to replace its ageing Walther P38. He assembled a team of pistol experts, patented a design within months, and the Glock 17 — his company's first gun — won the trials outright.

Adopted in 1982 as the Pistole 80, it broke with contemporary handguns on almost every count: a polymer frame instead of machined steel, a striker instead of a hammer, and a third the parts of comparable service pistols. Police and military forces worldwide followed the Austrian army's lead, and it remains one of the best-selling pistols on the market.

Model
Glock 17, chambered in 9 × 19 mm
Maker

01

17 / 35 · Polymer

Frame

Start with the part everything else hangs off. The frame is also the serialised component — legally it is the firearm, and the slide and barrel are just parts. Moulding it from polymer instead of machining it from steel is the decision the whole pistol is built around: roughly half the weight of a steel-framed service pistol, with the grip angle, magazine well and trigger guard all formed in a single shot.

Material
Nylon-based polymer with hardened steel rails and pins set into it
Contributes
Carries the fire control, guides the slide, holds the magazine
Why polymer
Weight, corrosion resistance, one moulded shape instead of many machining operations

01 / 15 · Nitrided steel

Slide

The moving mass. Firing drives it rearward, and on the way back it extracts and ejects the spent case, re-cocks the striker and compresses the recoil spring; on the way forward it strips the next round from the magazine and chambers it. It also houses the striker, extractor and sights, so most of the small steel parts in this model live inside it. The polymer cover plate at the rear closes the striker channel and holds those internals in.

Material
Steel, surface-hardened by nitrocarburising — the source of the near-black finish
Contributes
Cycles the action; carries striker, extractor and sights
Cover plate
Polymer, carries no load — which is why it is the part everyone engraves

03

02 · Nitrided steel

Barrel

The barrel does two jobs at once. It contains the pressure of the burning propellant and directs the bullet, and it also locks into the slide during firing, then tilts down to unlock as the two travel rearward together. That short-recoil, tilting-barrel arrangement is Browning's, worked out in the 1890s, and almost every modern service pistol still uses it.

Material
Steel, cold hammer-forged, then surface-hardened like the slide
Rifling
Polygonal — a rounded bore profile rather than sharp-cornered lands and grooves
Contributes
Contains pressure, spins the bullet, locks the action until pressure drops

03 / 04 · Spring steel

Recoil assembly

A spring on a rod, sitting in the space under the barrel. It absorbs the slide's rearward energy and gives it back to drive the slide forward into battery. Its rate is a tuning knob for the whole pistol: too weak and the slide batters the frame, too stiff and it will not cycle reliably. This model has the later dual-spring assembly — two nested rates on a captive rod.

Material
Spring steel, on a captive rod (steel or polymer depending on generation)
Contributes
Stores recoil energy, returns the slide, controls slide speed
Dual spring
Two rates soften the impulse and are gentler on the frame than one heavy spring

05

05 – 08 · Steel

Striker

There is no hammer. A spring-loaded striker runs down a sealed channel bored through the slide and hits the primer directly — nothing sits further inside the gun than this. Cycling the slide leaves it only partly cocked; the trigger pull draws it the rest of the way back before releasing it, so the mechanism is never sitting fully tensioned at rest.

Material
Steel striker and spring; polymer spacer sleeve keeps it centred
Spring cups
Two steel half-cups that capture the spring on the striker shaft
Contributes
Stores and releases the energy that sets off the primer

09 / 10 · Steel

Firing pin safety

A small spring-loaded plunger sitting directly across the striker's path, physically blocking it. It only lifts clear when the trigger bar rises during a deliberate trigger pull. Drop the pistol or strike it and the striker has nowhere to go. This is the second of three interlocked safeties, and like the others it has no lever — it is worked by the act of pulling the trigger.

Material
Steel plunger, spring steel coil
Contributes
Blocks the striker unless the trigger is genuinely being pulled
System
One of three: trigger safety, firing pin safety, drop safety

07

11 – 14 · Steel

Extractor group

A hook riding in a cut in the slide wall, with a plunger, spring and bearing behind it keeping constant outward tension on it. It catches the rim of the case and drags it out of the chamber as the slide travels back. Four small parts with an outsized effect on reliability — most feeding and ejection complaints trace back to this group.

Material
Steel hook, plunger and bearing; spring steel coil
Contributes
Grips the case rim and pulls the fired case clear of the chamber
Also
On many variants the extractor doubles as a loaded-chamber indicator you can feel

08

16 · Polymer

Sights

A blade at the front, a notched leaf at the rear, both dovetailed into the top of the slide. Factory sights are moulded polymer — genuinely the cheapest components in the pistol, and the first thing most owners replace. Steel and tritium night sights drop into the same cuts, and the rear notch is adjusted for windage by drifting it sideways in its dovetail.

Material
Polymer as standard; steel and tritium versions are common replacements
Contributes
Give the eye a repeatable reference for where the barrel is pointing
Mounting
Dovetail cuts, so the whole set is user-replaceable

09

26 · Polymer

Trigger with safety

Look closely at the trigger face and there is a second, smaller lever hinged into it. Unless that blade is pressed — which happens naturally when a finger is on the trigger — it bears against the frame and physically stops the trigger moving rearward. Catch the trigger on a holster edge or a drawstring and nothing happens. It is the first of the three safeties, and the only one you can see.

Material
Moulded polymer trigger and safety blade, steel pivot pin
Contributes
The interface, and the first safety in the chain
Pull weight
Set mostly by the connector, not by the trigger itself

10

24 – 26 · Steel

Trigger bar & connector

The trigger bar is a stamped steel strip running the length of the frame, and it does nearly all the mechanical work: it lifts the firing pin safety, drags the striker back, then rides up the angled face of the connector, which cams it sideways out from under the striker lug. A ramp on a stamping — that geometry is the entire trigger break. Change the connector's angle and you change the pull weight.

Material
Stamped and formed steel; spring steel trigger return spring
Contributes
Transmits the pull, releases the striker, resets the mechanism
Connector
Sets the release point and pull weight; swapping it is the standard trigger tune

11

23 · Polymer

Trigger housing & ejector

A polymer cage that drops into the frame and holds the fire-control parts in the right relationship to one another. Fixed into it is a steel ejector: a small spur the case strikes as the slide carries it rearward, flipping it out of the ejection port. The ejection patterns people argue about at length are largely a function of this one tab.

Material
Polymer housing with a steel ejector fixed into it
Contributes
Locates the trigger group and throws the spent case clear
Retention
Held by a single pin through the frame

22 / 28 / 29 / 34 · Steel

Locking block & pins

The locking block is the hardest-working piece of steel in the frame. The barrel cams against it to unlock and tilt on every single shot, so it absorbs the battering a polymer frame could never take — it is a large part of why a plastic-framed pistol works at all. Around it, three steel pins hold the assemblies in position, and those pins are the entire toolkit for stripping the fire control.

Material
Steel throughout; the block is hardened
Contributes
Times the barrel unlocking and transfers that load into the frame
Pins
Locking block pin, trigger pin, trigger housing pin

13

20 / 21 / 27 · Steel

Slide stop & takedown lever

Two levers that do unrelated jobs and get confused constantly. The slide stop is pushed up by the magazine follower when the last round is gone, catching the slide open so the empty chamber is visible. The takedown lever is the maintenance one: pull it down and the slide comes off the frame. No tools required, which is a large part of why police forces took to the design.

Material
Stamped and machined steel, with a spring steel leg on the takedown lever
Slide stop
Held open by the follower on an empty magazine
Takedown lever
Retains the slide on the frame

14

19 · Polymer

Magazine catch

A polymer block in the front strap of the grip that engages a notch in the magazine body. Press it and the magazine drops free. On later generations it is enlarged and can be reversed for left-handed shooters — a small change that says something about the design's age: the mechanism is untouched after forty years, the ergonomics have been revised repeatedly.

Material
Polymer, tensioned by a spring steel leg
Contributes
Retains the magazine and releases it on a thumb press
Later generations
Enlarged and reversible for left-handed use

15

30 – 33 · Polymer

Magazine

Five parts: body, spring, follower, insert and floorplate. The body is polymer over a steel liner, and that liner is the compromise that made polymer magazines viable at all — polymer alone flexes under load and lets the feed lips spread, which is how magazines cause malfunctions. The spring pushes the follower up the column, and the follower's rear tab is what lifts the slide stop when the last round leaves.

Material
Steel-lined polymer body and follower; spring steel coil
Contributes
Stores rounds under pressure and presents them to be stripped
On the name
Usually explained as the seventeenth patent Gaston Glock filed, not the capacity — the company describes the matching number as coincidence

00 · Brass

9 × 19 mm cartridge

Not a component of the pistol, but the reason every other component is shaped the way it is. The case is drawn brass — soft enough to expand against the chamber wall and seal it, springy enough to shrink back so the extractor can pull it out. Its pressure sets the barrel's wall thickness, the slide's mass and the recoil spring's rate. Change the cartridge and you redesign the gun around it.

Material
Drawn brass case, copper-jacketed lead bullet, primer in a brass cup
Role
The consumable. Everything else is dimensioned around it
Designation
9 × 19 mm Parabellum — 9 mm bullet, 19 mm case

Back together

Five materials,
one machine

Nothing here is exotic. Injection-moulded polymer where stiffness and weight matter more than hardness, surface-hardened steel wherever metal slides on metal or contains pressure, spring steel wherever energy needs storing, and brass for the one part that gets consumed. The engineering is entirely in which material goes where.

The pattern is worth noticing as it goes back together: the polymer parts are large, complex shapes made once in a mould. The steel parts are small, simple and heavily loaded. That division is the whole cost argument for a polymer-framed pistol, and it is why this layout has been copied by nearly every manufacturer since.
